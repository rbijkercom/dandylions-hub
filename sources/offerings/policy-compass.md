## Headline

Your work has already expertise. Now it needs someone who can contain the tension of complexity.

Some executive decisions are expensive to undo. The aim is to translate an abstract ambition into work a team can actually do, together with stakeholders. The Policy Compass is an instrument developed by the Dutch government to develop effective, applicable and human policies.

## Why this method

- The Policy Compass was designed for the most heavily scrutinised decisions there are: the kind that face formal internal challenge, public consultation, and questions in parliament. Its discipline is deliberately unglamorous. Establish the problem before the goal, the goal before the options, the options before the choice, with the question of who has a stake running through every one of those steps.
- That discipline isn't specialised knowledge. It's the thing that quietly disappears the moment a decision becomes urgent or political, in any organisation. Which is exactly why it holds up outside the context it was built for.

## The gap between the method and the practice

- The first independent evaluation of the Policy Compass found that the method is still applied relatively little, and often not in the way it was intended. Where it gets used as a form-filling exercise after the fact, the people involved tend to experience it as pointless and as an administrative burden.
- That's the trap, and it isn't unique to any one organisation. A required justification gets written once. If the choice of instrument sits before the analysis, that justification is by definition a reconstruction after the fact, and everyone in the room knows it. The fix isn't a better template. It's someone whose job it is to run the process in the order it was designed to run.

## What this is, and what it isn't

- **This is what it is for.** When the analysis genuinely precedes the choices, when the people involved take part, when the problem is contested, when more than one organisation or domain has to move.
- **It isn't compulsory fare.** It asks for thinking room to pick more than just the low-hanging fruit, and for the ability to carry tension productively for as long as there are no solutions.
- **The value sits at the front.** Designing the process, convening the right stakeholders, keeping the analysis honest while the pressure to name a solution builds, and translating a mission into something a team can work on week by week.

## Four reasons (not) to use the Policy Compass

**“We already know the solution.”**
In high stakes environments, urgency drives change. Most solutions are known before problems have a chance to be researched. The issue is not that organizations do not have solutions; it's that they often do not know what causes their problems.

**“A project leader is overhead.”**
A project manager is the cheapest and the most valuable at day one. In complex environments someone has to hold the tension while the analysis runs.

**“We can do this ourselves.”**
Any expert in their field has a clear idea of how to solve a problem. That knowledge is not the issue. The issue is that each knows only from their own perspective. Bringing in an external manager can mean the difference between implementing solutions and fighting symptoms.

**“We don't have time.”**
Time at the front is the cheapest time in the entire process, and skipping it is the best-documented way to extend the total lead time. A rushed preparation produces repair costs, missed opportunities, and relationships that harden.

## Who it's for

- Organisations facing a decision that will be hard to reverse: a scheme, a regulation, an investment, a redesigned service
- Directors and executives carrying a mission that doesn't sit neatly inside one department, one organisation, or one budget
- Partnerships, regions, and alliances where nobody has authority over everybody and the work still has to move
- Anyone handed a charged decision and no project structure to run it with

## What we can work on

- Holding the analysis open while the room pushes for a solution
- A problem definition that starts by the causes
- Genuinely weighing more than one option, including the uncomfortable ones
- Stakeholder mapping and co-creation that isn't a formality
- Working in short cycles towards clear interim results
- Translating an abstract mission into concrete work, roles, and rhythm

## How it works

1. **Opening conversation**: Preferably before anything is fixed. We map what's actually being asked, who has decided what already, and what still has room to move. This is the most valuable conversation in the whole process.
2. **Framing the mission**: A mission is not an assignment. An assignment specifies the outcome; a mission sets a direction and leaves the result open. Getting that framing right determines who needs to be at the table and what counts as success.
3. **Setting up and running the project**: Problem, intended goal, options, consequences, preferred option, with the stakeholder question running through all of them rather than bolted on at the end.
4. **Handover**: The point isn't that I stay. We agree from the start what has to be transferred, to whom, and by when, so that your organisation keeps the working method after the project closes.

## Common questions

**Do we have to use the Policy Compass?**
Following the Policy Compass is always a choice. Underneath it sit project management methods, as in any other project.

**We've already started. Is it too late?**
Maybe. What we can do is establish honestly what's still open and what has already been decided, and then decide whether the remaining room is worth a structured process.

**When is a project task-driven?**
A task sets a direction while the outcome stays open. A project delivers an agreed result. A task-driven project promises that the process will produce solutions that contribute to the task, with the outcome not determined in advance.
