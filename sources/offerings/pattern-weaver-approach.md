## Types of Problems

For adaptive work to run in parallel with technical work, it is important to know the distinctive features of both types of problems and the approach each one requires.

### Technical Problems

- Arise in complicated systems, where cause and effect are linear, knowable, and can be analyzed upfront. The focus is on execution through a well-defined plan, proven solutions, and clearly defined deliverables, milestones, and acceptance criteria.
- The project manager applies expertise, established processes, and formal authority over scope, schedule, and resources, following a sense–analyze–respond approach grounded in good practice.

Short form: sense – analyze – respond.

### Adaptive Problems

- Arise in complex systems, where cause and effect are non-linear, interdependent, and often only clear in hindsight. The focus is on shaping and aligning direction by probing unclear challenges, running safe-to-fail experiments, and building feedback loops that move stakeholders toward a shared outcome.
- The project manager needs to exercise influence without formal authority, leading and following peers and stakeholders and letting solutions emerge through a probe–sense–respond approach grounded in emergent practice.

Short form: probe – sense – respond.

## Where We Come In

- Most project teams struggle to surface, address, and organize around adaptive problems, which can't be solved by expertise alone and require team members and stakeholders to lead each other across boundaries.
- We bring our expertise to help project managers balance technical control with adaptive capacity, knowing when to plan and govern and when to probe, sense, and let solutions emerge.
- We help in building capacity that addresses both systemic and individual challenges at once, beyond specialization.
- Tomorrow's professional needs the capacity to hold complexity without flattening it, to bring the right people into the right process, and to build solutions that key stakeholders understand — and consent to, even when they disagree.

### What gets in the way of adaptive work?

- Competing loyalties are the biggest challenge a project manager faces when taking on adaptive problems, in government and in business alike. Every project sits inside a system of four loyalties that pull in different directions, and each one rewards a technical approach: a clear plan, a fixed process, and predictable outcomes.
- Under that pressure, project managers may hold back from surfacing tensions or giving feedback for fear of exposing the minister, bypassing the line, or stepping outside their legal mandate.
- This directly affects project outcomes. It dampens the feedback loops the system needs to learn and adapt, and it turns complex problems into complicated ones on paper while they remain complex in reality.

## Four Competing Loyalties

Competing loyalties are the biggest challenge a project manager faces when taking on adaptive problems, in government and in business alike. Every project sits inside a system of four loyalties that pull in different directions, and each one rewards a technical approach: a clear plan, a fixed process, and predictable outcomes.

### Loyalty to the Executive Top

The minister answers to parliament for everything officials do, just as a board answers to shareholders for everything the company does. Knowing this, people try to prevent errors and steer clear of risk wherever they can.

Seen from here, a safe-to-fail experiment looks like a political or reputational liability, so the project plan becomes a shield rather than a hypothesis to be tested.

### Loyalty to the End Users

The civil service oath commits officials to the general interest of society; a company makes a comparable promise to the customers it serves. Both ask you to treat people fairly, equally, and respectfully.

Citizens and customers are exactly where adaptive problems surface: messy, individual, and non-linear. When the plan and their reality diverge, the plan usually wins.

### Loyalty to the Direct Manager

Anything headed for parliament, or for the board, must first be approved by successive layers of management.

Each layer acts as a filter that simplifies, standardizes, and smooths out dissent. It is a hierarchy built for control rather than for learning.

### Loyalty to the Law and Governance

The same oath binds officials to the law, legal principles, and their codes of conduct; in a company the equivalent is governance, compliance, and internal policy.

Both are by design technical instruments: codified, general, and stable. When an adaptive problem meets a rigid rule, the rule wins and the person loses. In the childcare benefits scandal, the law lacked a hardship provision and the legislator gave far too little weight to proportionality.

## Shifting gears

- In the adaptive space, we want the project managers we coach to hold these loyalties consciously rather than letting them default to the path of least resistance. They will engage with their teams and stakeholders, promote dialogue and loyal dissent, and become more accessible to the team.
- They will learn to diagnose which type of problem they are facing and shift gears accordingly. That way they strike the right balance between technical control and adaptive capacity, serving minister or shareholder, citizen or customer, line, and law or governance, while addressing the challenges the system presents instead of avoiding them.
